# From a real design to photoreal stills and fly-throughs: what people actually do

Research date 20 Nov 2026. This builds on the earlier report, `AI architectural flythrough workflows.md`, and tests one question in both directions: do we need Higgsfield?

## The short answer

Higgsfield isn't the missing piece, but it isn't useless either. It's a storefront for other companies' video and image models (Kling, Veo, Seedance, Nano Banana, FLUX and others) with credits and presets on top. None of those models can take our camera path, and none of them lock to our geometry. Everything they do well, they do on top of a frame we've already rendered. So Higgsfield fits as a **finishing step for short social reels made from our own Cycles frames**. It shouldn't be the core of the showcase, and it doesn't touch the hero still's real gaps, which are lighting, styling and CPU render time. Its terms also let it train on anything we upload unless we sign an enterprise agreement. Every client design therefore needs written OK from the developer and architect before it goes near Higgsfield, which matches the owner rule we already have.

The honest case for the hunch is real, though. The thing our Cycles output lacks most for marketing is life: moving trees, water, people, a sun sweep, a camera that drifts. AI video adds that cheaply, at roughly A$9 a try for 10 seconds of 1080p on Higgsfield's Plus plan, against A$4,000 to A$25,000+ for a studio fly-through. For reels, that's worth having, and the account already exists.

AUD figures assume about A$1.53 per US$1 and are rounded. Credit and API prices move monthly, so check them before quoting.

## What the field looks like in practice

The earlier report's main finding holds up. Studios that sell real projects render the real model and use AI on top. Creators who go viral prompt the picture and aren't tied to a design. What's changed since then is how normal the middle path has become: a proper render or two, then AI to add motion between them.

**Studios.** KPF animates its own renders in Runway for pitches, replacing outsourced animation that used to take about two weeks per minute ([Runway, KPF](https://runway.com/customers/how-kpf-is-incorporating-ai-video-into-architectural-workflows)). Chaos, which owns V-Ray, Corona and Enscape, now ships image-to-video inside Veras 3.0, designed to turn finished renders into short animations from Revit, Rhino or SketchUp ([Chaos press release](https://www.chaos.com/press/chaos-unveils-several-new-ai-tools-led-by-veras-30-with-image-to-video-generator-for-aec); [AEC Magazine](https://aecmag.com/news/chaos-launches-ai-image-to-video-generator)). When the biggest renderer vendor builds "render, then animate with AI" into its product, that's the clearest sign of where pro practice sits.

**Educators and solo creators.** Nuno Silva, a Portugal-based archviz educator, publishes the most detailed public workflows. One builds an empty room and a furnished room with Nano Banana Pro, then uses them as the start and end frames of a Kling clip so the furniture appears to arrive. He also runs day-to-night transitions the same way, chained in a node tool called Fuser Studio ([RebusFarm summary](https://ie.rebusfarm.net/news/nuno-silva-insane-ways-to-use-nano-banana-pro-and-kling-for-interior-animations)). His head-to-head test found no single winner: Kling 3.0 for clean camera moves, Veo 3.1 for natural elements like wind ([RebusFarm summary](https://dk.rebusfarm.net/news/nuno-silva-which-ai-video-model-actually-wins)). Both are second-hand summaries of his videos; we didn't watch the originals, so his settings are unverified. A widely shared guide makes the same "two renders, AI between" argument on cost grounds: a 10 second move at 30 fps is 300 frames, and at about 15 minutes a frame that's days of rendering, while Kling's first-and-last-frame mode needs two ([AI Fire](https://www.aifire.co/p/ai-vs-render-farm-professional-archviz-workflow-2026)). That guide's claim that geometry stays stable is the author's, not a test. Studio Tim Fu's Midjourney, Photoshop and Kling sketches, covered in the earlier report, remain the prompt-first end of the range ([Architizer](https://architizer.com/blog/?p=203116)).

**Practitioners who sell against it.** Maverick Frame says AI video falls short for off-the-plan work because there's nothing real to photograph. It lists bent door frames, drifting furniture, wrong reflections and spec drift between runs ([Maverick Frame](https://maverickframe.com/blog/ai-video-for-real-estate/)). One correction to the earlier report: Maverick Frame is a London studio, not an Australian one. It sells both AI video and 3D animation, so its view isn't neutral.

**What we couldn't find.** We found no Reddit r/archviz thread with a practitioner's account of delivering client AI video, and no published benchmark of how well these models hold a straight facade or a window count through a camera move. Research groups have started measuring geometric consistency in generated video ([GeCo, arXiv 2512.22274](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.22274v2)), but nobody has run that on architecture and published the results. Vendor guides agree on the failure: mullions, columns and structural grids warp or "melt" as the camera moves, and image-to-video holds up much better than text-to-video ([learnarchitecture.net](https://learnarchitecture.net/artificial-intelligence/33845-ai-video-generation-tools.html); [Hailuo guide](https://hailuoai.video/pages/knowledge/troubleshooting-structural-warping-ai-architectural-video)). The Hailuo guide's numbers (vanishing-point drift under 5 degrees, 4 to 6 second clips) have no stated source.

## The six pipeline families

### (a) Traditional render, then AI finishing

The model is rendered properly in V-Ray, Corona, Unreal, D5, Twinmotion, Lumion, Enscape or Blender, and AI then improves the people, plants and surfaces, or upscales. Chaos AI Enhancer is the clearest product example. It exists to make flat CG people and plants photoreal inside Corona and V-Ray ([Digital Engineering](https://www.digitalengineering247.com/article/chaos-debuts-several-ai-tools)), and costs about 15 to 20 Chaos credits per operation ([aitoolsatlas](https://aitoolsatlas.ai/tools/veras/pricing), third-party, unverified). D5 Render 3.0 lists 15+ AI features, including AI post-production styling and scene matching, but we found no generative image-to-video in D5 ([D5](https://www.d5render.com/posts/d5-render-3-0-ai-rendering-design-workflow)).

Because the geometry comes from the render, accuracy is as good as the model. Consistency across shots is high, since each shot is a fresh render of the same scene. The cost is the render time and the artist's hours, and the AI step adds only a few dollars. This is the family our pipeline already belongs to.

### (b) Render or clay pass, then image-to-video

A rendered frame, or a start and end pair, goes into Kling, Veo, Seedance, Runway, Luma, Minimax or Wan, which invents the motion. This is where Higgsfield sits, and where KPF, Veras 3.0 and Nuno Silva sit too.

Accuracy holds well in the first and last frames, which are ours, and degrades in between. How much depends on how far the camera moves and how busy the facade is. Users report that Kling holds the opening seconds better than the end of a longer clip ([Virse, summarising Reddit](https://www.virse.ai/blog/kling-4-0-reddit-reviews), a commercial blog). Consistency across shots is moderate: each clip is a separate guess, and the same pair gives different in-betweens each run. Cost at API rates is about US$0.11 to US$0.14 a second for Kling 3.0 at 1080p and US$0.40 a second for Veo 3.1 Standard, so roughly A$1.70 to A$2.20 or A$6 per 10 second try ([CometAPI](https://www.cometapi.com/ai-video-api-pricing/); [invideo, citing Google's rates](https://invideo.io/blog/ai-video-model-pricing)). Rejection rates are the hidden cost; one user report cites 660+ credits spent to get one barely acceptable 5 second clip ([Virse](https://www.virse.ai/blog/kling-4-0-reddit-reviews), anecdotal). Generation takes minutes per clip.

The useful technique is chaining. Keep start and end frames close in colour, light and framing; the start frame sets the aspect ratio; the last frame of one clip becomes the first of the next ([Higgsfield, Kling guide](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/Kling-Start-End-Frames); [RunDiffusion](https://learn.rundiffusion.com/kling-3-0-pro-ai-tool/)).

### (c) Prompt-first, no model

Midjourney, Nano Banana or GPT Image make the still, Magnific upscales it, and Kling or Veo animates it. The look can be stunning because the AI picks the light, the weather and the camera. But nothing ties the window count, the floor plate or the finishes to the drawings, so it can't sell a real apartment. It's useful for mood boards and brand content only. The earlier report covers this fully, and nothing we found changes it.

### (d) ComfyUI with ControlNet

A render, plus its depth and line passes, goes through Flux or SDXL in img2img mode. ControlNet strength holds the structure and denoise strength sets how much the AI can change. The official Flux route uses the FLUX.1 Depth and Canny models ([ComfyUI Wiki](https://comfyui-wiki.com/en/tutorial/advanced/flux-controlnet-workflow-guide)). Higher strength follows the depth map more closely and middling strength leaves room to restyle ([Stable Diffusion Art](https://stable-diffusion-art.com/controlnet-comfyui/)). One guide suggests keeping the summed weights of stacked ControlNets at or below 2.0 ([RunComfy](https://www.runcomfy.com/comfyui-workflows/comfyui-flux-controlnet-depth-and-canny)). The earlier report's denoise bands (0.2 to 0.35 for a global polish, 0.4+ only on masked foreground) still stand.

For video, the same idea exists as Wan VACE. A cheap base animation is fed through with a depth control, and ComfyUI's own template notes depth preserves 3D structure better than edges ([Comfy hub, Video Restyle](https://comfy.org/hub/workflows/video-restyle-5931e9bdf9db/)). The 14B model gives 720p at higher fidelity but needs much more VRAM; the 1.3B model is for 480p ([Comfy hub, VACE](https://comfy.org/hub/workflows/wan2-1-vace-control-video-2652985596d8/)). We found no published architecture test of VACE, so its stability on long facades is unverified.

This is the most accurate AI family, because we feed it exact depth and line passes from Blender rather than estimated ones. It runs on hardware we control, so no third party trains on client designs. The cost is GPU time, under about A$8 per hero still, and the time is setup-heavy at first and then fast. It needs a rented GPU; it won't run usefully on the laptop.

### (e) Gaussian splats and NeRF

A splat is a cloud of soft 3D points that a browser can orbit in real time. For real estate it's mostly used to capture built spaces: Zillow SkyTour shipped splats in late 2025 and Apartments.com followed for exteriors ([LiDAR News](https://lidarnews.com/zillow-3d-tours-with-gaussian-splatting/); [RadianceFields](https://radiancefields.com/apartments-com-unveils-matterport-3d-exterior)). For unbuilt work, the route that keeps accuracy is training the splat from our own renders, covered in the earlier report ([RadianceFields](https://radiancefields.com/training-gaussian-splats-from-renders-not-photos)). World Labs shows floor plans turned into browser-explorable spaces ([World Labs](https://www.worldlabs.ai/labs/showcase/spatial-blueprints)), but those spaces are generated, so they carry the prompt-first accuracy problem. A practitioner notes that traditional rendering still gives more control when materials and lighting change through revisions ([Maverick Frame](https://maverickframe.com/blog/world-labs-marble-splats/)). Splats trained from our renders are as accurate as the renders, weak on glass and sky, and cheap: about US$5 a run on World Labs or free locally.

### (f) Architecture-specific AI tools

Veras, LookX, mnml.ai, ArkoAI, PromeAI, D5 AI and Chaos AI Enhancer fall into two groups. Veras works from the live model inside Revit, Rhino or SketchUp, and a geometry slider limits how far the AI may stray ([architecturelab](https://www.architecturelab.net/how-ai-reshaped-the-render-and-what-it-still-cant-build/)). User reports are split on whether it actually respects the geometry, as the earlier report found. Browser tools such as mnml.ai and LookX work from a flat screenshot, which gives the AI more freedom and less accuracy ([illustrarch](https://illustrarch.com/articles/architectural-rendering/76443-sketchup-ai-render-tools.html)). Veras Pro is listed at about US$29 a month billed annually, though Chaos's own page and trackers disagree ([aitoolsatlas](https://aitoolsatlas.ai/tools/veras/pricing); [PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/chaos)). We found no independent accuracy test of any of these tools. None takes IFC directly, and none can be scripted headless, so none fits an automated pipeline.

## Comparison

Accuracy means how well geometry, materials and window positions match the design. Cost and time are for one still, or one usable 10 seconds of video, at the rates cited above.

| Family | Accuracy | Consistency across shots | Cost | Time | Look |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Render, then AI finishing | High; as good as the model | High | Render time plus a few dollars of AI | Hours per still on CPU, minutes on GPU | As good as the dressing and lighting; AI fixes people and plants |
| (b) Render, then image-to-video | High at the two ends, drifts mid-clip | Moderate; each run differs | About A$2 to A$9 per 10 s try; several tries per keeper | Minutes per clip | Lively and cinematic; risk of warped mullions and drifting furniture |
| (c) Prompt-first | None; not tied to the design | Low | Cents per still, dollars per clip | Minutes | Often the most striking; atmosphere over truth |
| (d) ComfyUI with ControlNet | High with exact passes and low denoise | Good with fixed seed and prompt | Under about A$8 GPU per hero still | Setup days, then minutes | Adds photographic realism to a render; can repaint facades if pushed |
| (e) Splats from our renders | As accurate as the source renders | High; one baked scene | Free to about A$8 a run | Minutes to train on a GPU | Explorable, slightly soft, weak on glass and sky |
| (f) Architecture AI tools | Mixed; model-linked better than screenshot-based | Moderate | About US$29 to US$127 a month | Minutes | Polished; can take creative liberties |

## Higgsfield in detail

**What it gives access to.** Higgsfield's own pricing guide, verified October 2026, lists video models including Seedance 2.0 and 2.5, Veo 3 and Veo 3 Fast, Wan 2.5, Kling 3.0 and its own Speak and Genjutsu models. It lists image models including Nano Banana Pro, Nano Banana 2, FLUX.2 Pro and Max, Seedream 4.5 and 5.0, Kling O1 Image, GPT Image and Soul 2.0 ([Higgsfield pricing explained](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/higgsfield-pricing-explained)). Third-party reviews also list Veo 3.1 and Sora 2 ([Krea](https://www.krea.ai/blog/what-is-higgsfield-ai-pricing-free-plan-and-alternatives-in-2026)). Every one of those models is available elsewhere, so what Higgsfield adds is one subscription, presets and the workflow around them.

**Plans and credits.** Free has no monthly credits. Starter is US$15 for 200 credits, Plus US$49 for 1,000, Ultra US$129 for 3,000, and the Team plan is US$79 a seat for 1,000 credits ([Higgsfield pricing explained](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/higgsfield-pricing-explained)). The guide puts the Plus rate at about US$0.049 a credit. Seedance 2.0 costs 30 credits for 5 s at 720p, 60 at 1080p and 130 at 4K. Veo 3 with audio is about 58 credits for 8 s at 720p, and Nano Banana Pro is 2 credits a still (4 at 4K). A 10 second Seedance 2.0 clip at 1080p is therefore about 120 credits, roughly US$5.90 or A$9 per try. Higgsfield's guide doesn't list a Kling 3.0 credit cost. One third-party figure of about 8 credits per 5 s at 1080p is unverified ([Creatify](https://creatify.ai/blog/higgsfield-vs-kling-ai-quality-cost-per-second-and-which-to-use-for-ads-in-2026)). Subscription credits don't roll over ([Higgsfield Terms s.9.4](https://higgsfield.ai/terms)). The live pricing page didn't render for us, so check the plan table in the app before buying.

**Start frame, end frame and camera path.** Start and end frame control is included on Plus and Ultra but not on Free or Starter ([Higgsfield pricing explained](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/higgsfield-pricing-explained)). Kling's version supports 5 or 10 second clips at 1080p, takes its aspect ratio from the start frame, and offers more than twenty camera presets such as Flying Cam ([Higgsfield, Kling guide](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/Kling-Start-End-Frames)). Seedance 2.0 can copy the camera move from up to three reference videos totalling 15 seconds ([Novita guide](https://blogs.novita.ai/seedance-2-0-full-guide-to-multimodal-ai-video-generation/), third-party). That opens one interesting trick: render a fast, untextured EEVEE fly-through of our real camera path and give it to Seedance as the motion reference. Nobody has published a test of that for architecture, so it's untested. **We found no way to feed Higgsfield an actual camera path** (keyframes or a camera file). Movement comes from presets, prompt words or a reference video. Research models that re-render along a given trajectory exist, such as ReCamMaster, but they aren't products ([ReCamMaster](https://jianhongbai.github.io/ReCamMaster)).

**How well it holds architecture.** There's no Higgsfield-specific evidence. It runs the same models as everyone else, so the family (b) findings apply: good at the ends, drift in the middle, worst on busy facades and fast moves. Its own real-estate guidance recommends one gentle push-in or pan per clip ([Higgsfield, real estate](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/ai-real-estate-videos-from-photos)), which is the advice you give when bigger moves break.

**Commercial licence.** Higgsfield claims no ownership of inputs or outputs, doesn't restrict commercial use of outputs, and lets you sublicense outputs to clients ([Higgsfield Terms s.4.4](https://higgsfield.ai/terms), updated 23 Oct 2026). It makes no warranty about outputs and puts rights clearance on the user (s.13.2). Where a third-party model provider's acceptable-use policy is stricter, that policy governs (s.8).

**Training on uploads.** This is the deciding point for client work. The Terms say "Your Content, Inputs, and Outputs may be used" to train and improve Higgsfield's and its affiliates' models (s.4.4). The Privacy Policy says the same of uploaded media and prompts (s.2.3, [Privacy](https://higgsfield.ai/privacy), updated 31 Oct 2026). Higgsfield's own FAQ answers "Does Higgsfield train on my content?" with "Yes" ([Higgsfield blog](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/terms-of-use-privacy-policy-update)). There's no opt-out switch. Deleting content or the account stops future use, but anything already used in training stays in the trained models (Terms s.16.5). **Enterprise agreements are the exception: under them Higgsfield doesn't train on customer content** (s.4.4). So on our current plan, every client render we upload may become training data. That's a stronger reason than any quality concern to keep client designs off it without written OK, or to price an enterprise agreement first. We didn't check whether the underlying model makers' own APIs (Kling, Google's Veo via Vertex, fal) have friendlier data terms. That's worth checking before choosing a route for client work.

**Verdict on Higgsfield: reels and finishing, not core.** Use it to make short social clips that join two of our own Cycles keyframes, using Kling or Seedance start-and-end frames, and only after written OK for that project. Check each clip frame by frame against the drift check, and never use it for the scroll site or for anything a buyer reads as the design. It's also a fine place for brand content and mood work with no client design in it. It doesn't help the hero still, because the hero still's problems are lighting, styling and render speed, and an AI that repaints our image fixes those by making things up. If we ever want it for client work at volume, the enterprise no-training terms are the thing to ask about.

## Who's doing it in Australia, and for what

Australian studios mostly publish starting prices, not finished-job prices. Aurum Render read the websites of 42 Australian studios on 24 Oct 2026 and found only eight that publish prices. Their medians: exterior still A$600 (A$350 to A$1,000), interior still A$500 (A$345 to A$1,200) and aerial A$798 (A$350 to A$1,900). Only two publish film prices: A$6,500 a finished minute and A$65 a second ([Aurum Render survey](https://aurumrender.com.au/journal/3d-rendering-price-survey-australia/)). Aurum ran the survey and appears in it, and the survey leaves AI tools out. A developer guide gives much higher typical ranges for marketing work: hero exterior A$1,500 to A$6,000+, apartment interior A$800 to A$5,000, 60 second fly-through A$4,000 to A$25,000+, and four to six weeks for an animation ([Feasly](https://www.feasly.com.au/guides/architectural-rendering-australia-developer-guide)). Those ranges are unsourced. The two sources together suggest the earlier report's figures are a mid-to-high band, while entry starting prices sit lower.

The closest local competitor to what we're building is reIMG. It says it's Australian, takes SketchUp, IFC or CAD, and runs AI at the core with a person directing each job and output checked against the client's model. Its exploration packages start at A$600 + GST and finished campaign sets at A$2,000 + GST. It puts the market's "AI-powered done-for-you" tier at A$30 to A$150 an image ([reIMG](https://reimg.io/blog/architectural-rendering-australia-complete-guide/)). That's a vendor page, and we found no ABN or address on it. Binyan Studios, the best-known Australian archviz studio, appears to use AI video built on its own CGI base scenes, but our only source is a LinkedIn profile snippet ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/louise-richardson/)), so treat that as unverified. Both reIMG and Feasly say purely generative AI isn't suitable for council or tribunal photomontages.

We found no Australian studio publishing a price for AI fly-through video specifically.

## Recommended stack for accurate heroes and fly-throughs of real projects

The goal splits into three outputs, and each wants a different tool.

**Hero stills.** Keep IFC into headless Blender Cycles; it's family (a), the one the pros use. Move final renders and lighting iteration to a rented GPU, because CPU render time is what stops us iterating light, and light is the biggest open gap. Then add the family (d) pass: ComfyUI with Flux Depth and Canny, driven by Blender's exact depth and line passes, at low denoise, on the same rented GPU, behind the existing drift and finishes checks. This adds photographic realism without sending client geometry to anyone. Styling and an art-direction pick stay human.

**The scroll site's fly-through.** Keep Cycles frame sequences for anything a buyer scrolls through. They're exact, they don't flicker, and they're what the drift check can prove. On a GPU, a few hundred frames is an overnight job rather than a week. The open, geometry-locked experiment worth one day is Wan VACE: a cheap EEVEE animation with a depth control, to see whether it can add realism to a long move without drifting.

**Social reels.** This is Higgsfield's slot. Render two or three Cycles keyframes per shot, then chain Kling 3.0 or Seedance start-and-end clips between them on the Plus plan. Budget about A$9 per 10 s try and several tries per keeper. Upload only after written OK from the developer and architect, and check every clip frame by frame. The "EEVEE fly-through as a Seedance motion reference" idea is worth one small paid test on a non-client model once the owner approves the credit spend.

**Optional explore moment.** Use a splat trained from a Cycles orbit of one interior, as the earlier report proposed.

What this changes in the earlier report: it adds a named place for Higgsfield (reels, permission-gated), a hard reason to keep client files off it (training by default), and Veras 3.0 as proof that "render then animate" is now mainstream. It also corrects Maverick Frame's location and lowers the floor of the Australian price range. Nothing we found makes prompt-first or architecture-specific browser tools suitable for buyer-facing images.
